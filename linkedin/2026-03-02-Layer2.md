You hit send on a message to someone on your local network. It feels instant. But in the milliseconds before anything moves, your machine does something most people never think about.

It does not just fire data onto the network. It listens first.

Is anyone else transmitting? If yes, it waits. If the medium is clear, it wraps your data into a frame, stamps its own MAC address as the source, the destination device's MAC as the recipient, and sends it out.

But here is where it gets interesting. Your machine does not know if the frame arrived. So it listens while it transmits. If two devices send at the exact same moment and their signals collide, both detect the crash, stop immediately, wait a random amount of time, and try again. Neither one gets priority. They just roll the dice and whoever waits less goes first.

The frame itself is not just raw data either. It carries:

✅ A preamble to sync the receiving device's clock before data arrives.
✅ Source and destination MAC addresses for local delivery.
✅ An EtherType field identifying what is inside, IPv4, IPv6, ARP.
✅ A checksum at the end. If the frame was corrupted in transit, it gets silently dropped.

This is how two machines on the same network actually talk to each other. Layer 2 does not know about IPs. It does not know about the internet. It only has one job: get this frame from one device to another locally, without errors.

Everything else in networking is built on top of that one job working perfectly.

Did you ever think about what is happening at this level when you send something? 👇
